VTOL UAV Wing Design

Aerospace Engineering · Structural analysis, FEA simulation, and production-ready wing design

Engineered the optimal fixed wing for a VTOL (Vertical Take-Off and Landing) UAV — balancing aerodynamic efficiency, structural integrity, and real-world manufacturability. The project spanned the full design cycle: from geometry and load analysis through FEA simulation, then into DFM and DFA to make the wing producible at scale.

Overview

Wing Design Goals

The wing needed to satisfy competing demands — maximum lift-to-drag ratio for efficient cruise flight, sufficient structural stiffness to survive gust loads, and a geometry compatible with the VTOL transition regime where the aircraft accelerates from hover to forward flight. Every design decision was evaluated against all three constraints simultaneously.

Aerodynamic Efficiency

Airfoil selection and planform geometry optimized for the UAV's cruise speed and altitude envelope, minimizing induced and profile drag.

Structural Integrity

Wing designed to carry flight loads plus safety factors across all maneuver and gust load cases without permanent deformation or failure.

VTOL Compatibility

Geometry accounts for the transition regime — the wing must generate usable lift at low airspeeds while the rotors offload during acceleration.

Structure

Internal Structure

The wing uses a conventional spar-and-rib construction with skin panels — optimized for stiffness-to-weight ratio. Spar placement was determined by the spanwise shear and bending moment distribution under maximum load cases. Rib spacing was driven by skin buckling limits and manufacturing constraints.

Structural Elements

  • Main Spar: Primary load-carrying element positioned at the aerodynamic center — carries the bulk of the bending moment across the span.
  • Ribs: Maintain airfoil profile under load and transfer aerodynamic pressure to the spar. Spaced to prevent skin buckling.
  • Skin: Carries shear loads and contributes to torsional stiffness. Thickness selected against buckling criteria.
  • Material: Composite layup — high specific stiffness and strength with low weight penalty versus aluminum.
Load Analysis

Load Capacity

The wing was sized against a full set of load cases: level cruise, pull-up maneuver, gust loads, and asymmetric loading. A safety factor was applied to limit load to arrive at ultimate load, and the structure was verified not to yield or buckle at ultimate.

Load Cases

  • Limit Load: Maximum load expected in service — the structure must not permanently deform under this load.
  • Ultimate Load: Limit load × safety factor — the structure must not fracture or collapse.
  • Gust Loads: Sharp-edged and graded gust models applied across the flight envelope to establish worst-case spanwise bending.
  • Maneuver Loads: Symmetric pull-up and push-over maneuvers at the limit load factor.
  • Torsion: Pitching moment loads verified against skin shear and tip twist limits.
Simulations

FEA Analysis

Finite Element Analysis was used to validate the analytical sizing — confirming stress distributions, deflection under load, and identifying any stress concentrations at spar-rib joints and root attachments. The simulation results drove several geometry refinements before the design was locked.

Simulation Results

  • Stress Distribution: Von Mises stress verified to remain below material yield strength at ultimate load across all structural members.
  • Deflection: Tip deflection under limit load confirmed within acceptable bounds for control surface effectiveness.
  • Buckling: Linear buckling analysis on skin panels — all eigenvalues above 1.0 at limit load with margin.
  • Stress Concentrations: Root attachment and spar-rib interfaces checked and fillets sized to reduce peak stress.
Design for Manufacturing

DFM — Making It Buildable

Structural optimality means nothing if the part can't be made. The wing geometry was reviewed against real manufacturing constraints — composite layup access, mold complexity, minimum radii, and ply drop-off schedules. Features that would require complex tooling or multi-step bonding were simplified without sacrificing structural performance.

DFM Considerations

  • Mold Access: Internal geometry designed so that composite layup can be completed without blind corners or inaccessible surfaces.
  • Minimum Radii: All internal corners sized above the minimum bend radius for the composite fabric to prevent ply bridging and voids.
  • Ply Schedules: Layup sequence defined for balanced and symmetric laminate — eliminating residual thermal warping after cure.
  • Fastener Locations: Root attachment bolt pattern designed for standard drill fixtures and inspection access.
Design for Assembly

DFA — Making It Assemblable

The wing was designed so that final assembly is fast, repeatable, and doesn't require specialized fixtures beyond a simple jig. Spar insertion, rib bonding sequence, and skin closure were all planned as a linear workflow with no backtracking or rework steps.

DFA Considerations

  • Assembly Sequence: Spar → ribs → lower skin → internal components → upper skin. Each step is accessible before the next begins.
  • Self-Locating Features: Ribs and spar include datum features that register against each other, eliminating the need for complex jigging.
  • Part Count: Minimized through consolidation — several rib and bracket features merged into single parts where manufacturing allowed.
  • Inspection Points: Critical bond lines and fastener holes accessible for visual inspection at each assembly stage before closure.
